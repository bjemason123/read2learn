# Read to Learn Should Deepen, Not Widen

**Read to Learn should deepen its single-goal loop before it widens into a curriculum builder.** The research finds no verified product that combines a learner-stated goal, the learner's own reading and a living, finite plan, but this whitespace is a hypothesis: the competitive scan was limited, and a direct competitor may exist that it did not find. The learning-science evidence supports a short list of mechanisms: specific goals with a learner-defined test of "done", retrieval practice with spaced review, and guardrailed AI. It is weak or absent for the things a "curriculum builder" usually adds, such as elaborate planning UIs, learner-drawn prerequisite graphs, streaks and AI-generated plans. The shipped product has a goal, first-class questions, ordered reading items, tagged notes and a concept graph, but no time model, no outcome or completion condition, no retrieval and no export beyond print. The PRD (last edited 2026-08-25) is stale against the code on at least eight points, including authentication, the questions model, routes, ordering and the concept graph. The recommended path is small and additive. Scaffold goal framing, add a learner-defined evidence-of-achievement to each question, add a light retrieval prompt tied to those questions, and add a weekly time budget with pace-based projection. Defer modules, prerequisite DAGs, standards work and AI curriculum drafting. Refresh the PRD first, because the product has outrun its own scope document.

A note on evidence tags. The learning-science notes label each finding **[V]** (core numbers seen in primary-record abstract text), **[S]** (secondary source only, numbers need checking) or **[B]** (background knowledge, not re-verified, URL from memory). Strength labels are STRONG, MODERATE or WEAK. Those tags are carried through below, and a caveats register appears before the recommendations.

## The whitespace is a goal that owns a finite reading plan, and it remains unproven

The market splits into three layers that rarely meet. Roadmap and path products give structure but start from the provider's catalogue: roadmap.sh lists 97 roadmaps ([roadmap.sh](https://roadmap.sh/)), and LinkedIn Learning paths are curated course playlists ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/learning/answer/a703777)). Reading and knowledge tools give capture and review but no curriculum: Readwise Full costs **$9.99/month billed yearly** and includes Daily Review and, on Highlights plans, flashcards ([Readwise pricing](https://readwise.io/pricing)). AI tutors give session-scoped Socratic explanation: Gemini Guided Learning launched 6 August 2025 with questions, quizzes and multimodal responses, and its launch post does not mention learning plans ([Google blog](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/guided-learning/)). ChatGPT Study Mode is free on all plans ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-launches-study-mode-for-chatgpt-while-education-users-are-told-to-wait-and-learn-later/)). Prerequisite graphs exist mainly in expert-authored systems such as Metacademy, whose sources date from about 2014 ([hunch.net](https://hunch.net/?p=2714)). Users hand-assemble Zotero plus Notion or Obsidian queues to fill the gap ([Medium: PhD Tips](https://medium.com/@GenShanahan/phd-tips-the-project-and-knowledge-management-template-a7f67e94d9ae)), and one Notion-tracker critique calls most templates "glorified course lists" lacking active recall ([DEV](https://dev.to/lux_seminare/how-to-build-learning-tracker-in-notion-3148)).

Against that backdrop, the researchers identify six candidate gaps, all hypotheses: goal-first onboarding; a finite plan with an explicit time budget and WIP limit; plan re-flow or "reduce scope" after missed time; a concept graph and notes drawn from the learner's own reading; goal-scoped retrieval prompts instead of an unbounded flashcard queue; and data portability. The re-flow idea answers a documented pain point. Anki users describe missed days compounding into an unclearable backlog and then quitting, and one forum user proposed a "Backlog Recovery Planner" ([Anki forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/proposal-backlog-recovery-planner-to-prevent-user-churn-burnout/70487)). That evidence is anecdotal and feature-request grade, not measured churn, and one commenter reports quitting Anki simply because he disliked it ([Japanese Level Up](https://japaneselevelup.com/?p=26463)). The most useful structural finding is that free or cheap tutors (Khanmigo is listed at $4/month, [Khanmigo](https://khanmigo.ai/pricing)) mean a new tool cannot win on explanation. Differentiation has to rest on persistence, ownership and structure.

The competitive evidence is thinner than it looks, and the report should not over-claim it. The sources for most pricing are aggregator or vendor blogs, and several figures conflict (Anki iOS pricing, and one June 2026 source's "no Reader-only plan" claim against Readwise's own page listing one). Not verified at all: Duolingo's adaptive path, Zotero, Obsidian, Notion, Mochi and Goodreads official features, roadmap.sh custom roadmaps or progress tracking, and Metacademy's current status. OpenAI and Anthropic launch details rest on press because the official pages were not retrievable. NotebookLM, which now offers flashcards, quizzes and a Learning Guide with saved progress ([Chrome Unboxed](https://chromeunboxed.com/massive-notebooklm-update-adds-ai-powered-flashcards-quizzes-and-a-personal-tutor-mode/)), is the nearest adjacent threat and was not tested hands-on, so whether it supports a persistent goal-scoped plan is unknown. A targeted Product Hunt, Reddit and Hacker News check for 2024 to 2026 consumer "AI learning roadmap" launches was not completed. There is also no evidence of willingness to pay among time-poor adult self-improvers and no market sizing, and the PRD itself admits "no direct user interviews or market data" ([PRD §2](../docs/reading-curator-mvp.prd.md)).

On why people abandon plans, the best evidence is MOOC-specific. A qualitative study of 34 MOOC learners titled "Time is the bottleneck" found four dropout factors, with time management foremost ([Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/?id=249334)), and adult learners define "completion" differently from one another ([Online Learning Journal](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/889)). No rigorous study of abandoned personal reading lists or learning plans was found. The "collector's fallacy" and "read-later graveyard" are practitioner lore ([DEV](https://dev.to/the_nortern_dev/your-read-later-list-is-a-graveyard-it-is-time-to-stop-hoarding-388g)).

## The evidence backs specific goals and retrieval, and is cool on elaborate planning

The learning-science review converges on an ordering: outcomes first, then evidence of achievement, then activities (Wiggins and McTighe's backward design, Biggs' constructive alignment, revised Bloom). These are design heuristics with WEAK efficacy evidence, no RCT or meta-analysis located, and all three are **[B]**. The stronger evidence concerns goals themselves. In a 430-study meta-analysis of self-regulated learning (N = 90,380), goal level, persistence, effort and self-efficacy had the strongest effects on learning, while planning, monitoring and help-seeking showed no significant relationship **[V, STRONG but correlational]** ([Sitzmann & Ely 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/)). Among 4,831 learners in six MOOCs, goal setting and strategic planning were associated with attaining personal goals **[V, MODERATE]** ([Kizilcec et al. 2017](https://www.ing.uc.cl/?p=48917)). These two results partly conflict on planning. The researchers' reconciliation is that value lies in specific, challenging goals and persistence, not in elaborate plan artefacts, so a heavy planning UI should not be assumed to help. No experiment tests learners writing their own objectives against receiving them, and nothing tests Understanding by Design as an intervention.

Retrieval and spacing are the best-supported mechanisms in the whole dossier. Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing and distributed practice "high" utility and summarisation, rereading and highlighting "low" **[V for the high and low sets; the moderate set is B]** ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Karpicke and Blunt found retrieval practice produced about **50% better performance a week later** than elaborative study with concept mapping, even on a test that required drawing concept maps, and students predicted the opposite **[V, STRONG, undergraduates]** ([Purdue summary](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html)). A testing-effect meta-analysis reports an average g = 0.50, rising to 0.73 with feedback against 0.39 without **[S, numbers need checking]** ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)). Concept mapping helps relative to lower-activity controls (g = 0.58 overall; creating maps g = 0.72 against studying given maps g = 0.43) **[S]** ([Schroeder et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9)), but most controls were reading or lecture, not retrieval. The implication is that a concept graph is a supported generative activity but not a substitute for recall. Interleaving is domain-dependent (g = 0.42 overall, but g = -0.39 for word-based materials) **[V]** ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)), so mixing topics should not be a default for reading ordering.

Two findings caution against over-engineering learner control and sequencing. A meta-analysis of learner control in educational technology found an average effect near zero (g = 0.05, 18 studies) **[S]** ([EdWeek summary of Karich et al. 2014](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05)), and learners often prefer restudy to self-testing and misjudge their own mastery **[S, via secondary summaries of Kornell & Bjork and Bjork et al.]**. So Read to Learn's manual ordering is neutral on learning but probably positive for ownership, and it needs scaffolds (self-test prompts, a "what do I already know" check) rather than more control. The expertise-reversal effect **[S]** ([Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)) means structure helps novices and can hurt experienced learners, so default guidance should be tuned to a declared familiarity level. No evidence was found that novices can author an accurate prerequisite graph or that "suggested reading order" tools help, so the concept graph is better justified as sense-making and gap-spotting than as a sequencing engine.

On time and planning, the effects are real but smaller than commonly claimed. Implementation intentions showed d = 0.65 across 94 tests in the 2006 meta-analysis **[V]** ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)). A 2024 re-analysis reportedly gives bias-corrected figures of d = 0.35 or d = 0.15 depending on estimator **[S, from a glossary page, conflicting, unverified]** ([glossary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions)), so promise no large gains. Mental contrasting with implementation intentions has g = 0.336 across 21 studies **[V]** ([Wang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)). Planning prompts raised HarvardX completion 29% in one field experiment **[V]** ([Yeomans & Reich 2017](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137973)), but a multi-site scale-up improved early engagement and not final completion **[V, check against the paper]** ([Kizilcec et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)). The planning fallacy (students predicted thesis completion in about 34 days against about 56 actual, recalled from memory) is **[B]** ([Buehler et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366)). Habit formation took a median of about 66 days, and a single missed day did not materially matter **[V, n = 96]** ([Lally et al. 2010](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=8902)). Together these argue for a pace-based projection rather than a stated optimistic deadline, tolerance of missed days, and no loss-framed streaks. For streaks specifically, no evidence was retrieved either way.

AI-assisted learning is where the evidence is most decisive on what not to build. In a randomised field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school maths students, unrestricted GPT-4 improved practice performance by 48% but left the group **17% worse on the later unaided exam** than students who never had access, while a guardrailed tutor largely removed the harm **[V, MODERATE-STRONG, one school and subject]** ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html)). A Harvard physics crossover trial found an AI tutor giving one step at a time more than doubled median learning gains over in-class active learning **[S, elite sample, authors caution against generalising]** ([Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)). A coding-course preprint found weaker-prior-knowledge learners learned less and overestimated their benefit **[S, preprint]** ([Lehmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)). The supported roles are critiquing a goal, generating retrieval prompts after the learner attempts, hints on request, and pace flags. The unsupported or risky roles are generating the whole curriculum and order, summarising in place of reading, writing the learner's notes, and giving answers before an attempt. No evidence exists on AI-generated curricula as such, so that conclusion is inference.

## A small additive data model fits the stack; standards and heavy graphs do not

The technical research recommends borrowing vocabulary from standards, not adopting any of them. CASE's transferable idea is a typed edge table instead of a hard-wired tree ([IPSJ article](https://ftp.ipsj.or.jp/magazine/9faeag0000005al5-att/6210peta.pdf)). Knowledge space theory's "outer fringe" (items outside the learner's state whose prerequisites are met) is the same as "all prerequisites done, itself not done" on a prerequisite DAG, and so translates directly to a "next action" query ([arXiv 0803.4030](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.4030)). Metacademy's separation of concept, prerequisite and resource maps to outcome, link and reading item. CTDL, Open Badges 3.0 (final release shown as issued 29 June 2026, [1EdTech](https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0/)), xAPI/cmi5, LTI and ESCO/O*NET are judged overkill for a single-learner tool, with schema.org `LearningResource` property names (`timeRequired`, `teaches`, `competencyRequired`) worth using only for an optional export ([schema.org](https://schema.org/LearningResource)). Several standards questions stayed open: whether CASE has a `precedes` association type, whether CTDL has a competency-prerequisite property, whether OB 3.0 permits self-issued credentials, ESCO and O*NET licensing terms, and LTI and Bloom's taxonomy (no source retrieved for either). Bloom levels are treated as general knowledge, not a cited finding.

The proposed schema extends `Goal` as the curriculum root and adds `Module` (ordered milestone), `Outcome`, an `OutcomeLink` prerequisite edge table validated acyclic in application code, an `Activity` join carrying time estimates, `NoteOutcome`, `ReviewCard`/`ReviewLog` and `CurriculumDraft`/`DraftItem`. Time projection is `today + ceil(sum of undone estMinutes / weeklyMinutes)` weeks, and the next-action query is a topological pass. On SQLite, Prisma enums map to TEXT and are not enforced by the database, so values must be validated in TypeScript ([Prisma SQLite docs](https://www.prisma.io/docs/orm/overview/databases/sqlite)). The sketch is **unvalidated against Prisma 7.9.1**, and Prisma's self-relation and many-to-many behaviour on SQLite was not checked in the docs. The repo also has an important wrinkle that the sketch only partly reflects. `Question` is already a first-class row with a many-to-many link to `Note`, so the existing question set is a de facto objectives list, and the new `Outcome` table partly duplicates it. This report's own judgement, not the researchers', is that the first step should extend `Question` with a learner-defined completion criterion and status, and defer the `Outcome`, `Module` and `OutcomeLink` tables until usage shows learners want that granularity. That judgement is consistent with the Sitzmann and Ely planning null and with the absence of evidence that learner-authored prerequisite structure works.

For review scheduling, the notes favour FSRS via `ts-fsrs` (MIT) because it outputs a recall probability that can rank due items. Benchmark figures for FSRS against SM-2 conflict between sources (SM-2 log loss 0.354 against FSRS-5 0.291 on one page and FSRS-4 0.327 on another), the primary `srs-benchmark` repository was not opened, and no figure should be quoted ([Expertium benchmark](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)). The `ts-fsrs` README now states that v6 needs Node 24+ and defaults to FSRS-7 with an async `DefaultScheduler()` ([ts-fsrs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/ts-fsrs)). That differs from older examples, and the exact API must be confirmed from the installed package. The repo's local Node is v26.13.0 but `@types/node` is `^20`, so pin `engines.node`. SM-2 remains a roughly 20-line zero-dependency fallback with no probability output. The suggestion to persist the scheduler card as version-tagged JSON plus denormalised `due` and `state` columns, and to treat a flat 0.5 to 1 minute per due card as a review-time cost, are engineering inferences, and the minute figure is an unsourced assumption.

The AI drafting design is a four-stage pipeline: Claude produces a flat, ID-referenced JSON draft under a schema; the server resolves resource suggestions against OpenAlex, Crossref and Open Library and promotes only verified matches to reading items; the server validates acyclicity, sizes and budget; and the learner accepts, edits or rejects each item. Claude's structured outputs support this shape (`output_config.format` with `json_schema`, but no recursive schemas, no numeric or string-length bounds, and limits of 24 optional parameters and 16 union-typed parameters, [Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). The rule that the model must never output DOIs, ISBNs or URLs as facts is an engineering inference, since no source on LLM citation-hallucination rates was retrieved. The bibliographic APIs are volatile. From 21 July 2026 Crossref list requests are limited to 1 request per second (public) or 3 (polite), applied per `mailto` address ([Crossref community post](https://community.crossref.org/t/refining-rest-api-limits-for-improved-stability-and-reliability/16137)), which conflicts with the older access page that predates the change. OpenAlex has a free key, a 10 requests per second keyless limit, CC0 data, and a pacing change affecting accounts created after 3 November 2026 ([OpenAlex rate limits](https://help.openalex.org/how-to-use-the-api/rate-limits-and-authentication)). Open Library allows 1 request per second unidentified or 3 identified, with a search schema "not guaranteed to be stable" ([Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/developers/api)). Not checked: Anthropic tool-use and pricing pages, model choice and cost, OpenAlex daily budget, and the data licences for Crossref metadata and Open Library inside exports.

On views, the notes recommend building in descending value-for-effort: a "Next up" card, a module list with per-module progress, a time-budget strip, then a layered prerequisite graph. React Flow's guidance favours dagre for trees, with elkjs about 1.46 MB against about 40 kB for dagre ([React Flow](https://reactflow.dev/learn/layouting/layouting)). For 100 nodes or fewer, a hand-rolled layered layout in the existing dependency-free SVG is feasible, and React Flow's React 19.2 compatibility was not verified. Server-rendered lists and forms for core flows suit the repo's recent "works without JS" fix (commit `2c58d9e`). Export is recommended as one versioned JSON document plus a Markdown rendering, with CSL JSON, BibTeX, OPML and schema.org JSON-LD as optional lossy views. That section is mostly design judgement, with no primary sources fetched for OPML, CSL, BibTeX or Pandoc behaviour.

The PRD defines the MVP as: create a goal, add reading items, update the list across sessions, mark progress ([PRD §9](../docs/reading-curator-mvp.prd.md)). `Goal` is the nearest analogue, a flat container with no status, completion condition, deadline, time budget or parent entity ([schema](../prisma/schema.prisma)). Neither document has a multi-goal programme, an outcome taxonomy or a calendar. The inventory identifies at least eight places where the PRD is stale against the code or against itself. Each is tagged by what was verified. Authentication: the PRD says "no authentication", while the code has a `User` model, `Goal.userId`, `Tag.userId`, `src/proxy.ts` redirecting to `/login`, and signup and logout routes (commit `8b73e2a`). Questions: PRD §10 and §7 still describe newline-delimited text and call entities "still open", while `Question` rows with note links exist and #22 is closed. Entity and tag model: the PRD omits `User` and `Question`, calls `Tag` globally unique rather than unique per user, and documents `Event` without `userId`. Routes: `/graph`, `/login`, `/signup` and logout are missing from the PRD route map. Concept graph: the PRD defers concept mapping and says no landing claim promises it, yet commit `23039f1` (PR #37) shipped `/graph` with a nav link, with no PRD mention or decision-log entry. Ordering: PRD §5 says the list is "unordered" while §11 and the code have `position` ordering with up/down buttons (commit `e4b2188`). Decisions Log: the last bullet still says technical approach is unresolved because "the repository has no application code", contradicting §10. Phase table against MoSCoW: deferral/backlog is "Could" in the MoSCoW table but shipped per §11. The supporting design spec for notes still says "implementation plan not yet written" though the feature shipped. Items moved between goals, print-with-notes and first-class questions are also absent from "Also shipped".

Four further observations concern code health and the issue tracker. The telemetry is still write-only: roughly 15 `recordEvent` call sites and no readers, so the PRD's primary metric of visit and update frequency remains uncomputed (#21 open). The graph's "← Back to goals" link points to the landing page, repeating the #18 defect on a new page, and no issue tracks it. #39 (graph unreadable in dark mode) is open, and the inventory's explanation, hard-coded `#c7b8f0` and `#cfcfcf` edge colours, is an inference not confirmed at runtime. Issue #38 ("No available method of updating notes") is open though the code has inline editing; its true status is unclear from the repo alone. Finally, `moveReadingItemToGoal` updates only `goalId` and `position`, so a moved item's notes can stay linked to questions on the old goal. That risk comes from reading the code, no run test or covering test was found, and it should be verified before being treated as a bug. The inventory did not run the app at runtime and did not read tests, migrations or auth internals in detail.

Two deeper tensions sit between the docs and any curriculum ambition. First, REQ treats "imposing a reading sequence" and "generating the learner's understanding" as permanent non-goals (REQ §8), whereas the PRD frames sequencing as merely deferred. That means an AI-generated curriculum conflicts with REQ's stated principle that the learner authors goals and maps, although REQ does allow AI to propose candidate map nodes (FR-109), prerequisite material (FR-67) and a default order on request (FR-101). Second, the learning-science evidence largely validates REQ's design stance (learner control without cognitive-load hand-off, retrieval as spine, constructed maps over studied ones), so REQ is better aligned with the literature than the thin PRD. The PRD's deferral of retrieval is therefore its biggest divergence from the evidence, though the PRD's reason is defensible: the MVP tests whether learners return to and maintain a plan, not whether they learn. The shipped graph is also weaker than REQ's evidence base implies. It is machine-derived from tags and undirected, so it falls on the "studying a map" side of the g = 0.43 against g = 0.72 contrast that REQ itself cites **[S]**, and the learner constructs nothing. The code does not enforce REQ's science-only scope either.

## What the evidence does not settle

Several caveats shape how hard to lean on this research. Almost no learning-science evidence is specific to adult self-directed learners reading their own curated material. Most comes from students in courses, MOOCs or labs, so transfer is an assumption. Abstract-level and secondary sources underlie many numbers, with items tagged **[B]** needing verification before publication. No pooled expertise-reversal effect size, no scaffolding-fading evidence and no evidence on retrieval-based concept mapping against retrieval practice were retrieved. No evidence was found on time-budgeting tools for reading lists, the planning-fallacy remedy of reference-class forecasting, streak mechanics, or whether backlog size predicts abandonment. The self-regulated-learning goal-setting literature of Locke and Latham, Bandura and Schunk, Zimmerman, Grow, Knowles, Hulleman and Slamecka is **[B]** throughout. The AI-and-learning evidence includes several preprints (Lehmann, Fan, Kosmyna), and no AI-and-learning meta-analysis was retrieved or verified. The Reich and Ruipérez-Valiente MOOC completion figure (generally below 10%) is second-hand, from a blog summary. For the technical material, none of the Next.js-specific behaviour was verified from current Next.js documentation, which matters because the repository's `AGENTS.md` warns that this Next.js version has breaking changes. The schema sketch is unvalidated, the `ts-fsrs` v6 API is unconfirmed, and the vendor API terms (Crossref 21 July 2026, OpenAlex 3 November 2026) changed within months of the research date of 2026-11-07, so re-check them before implementation. Beyond the market and evidence gaps, the PRD itself carries unresolved TBDs (§2 gap and §6 target thresholds), and no issue exists for time budgeting, milestones, outcomes, spaced review, AI help or data export.

What to avoid is clearer than what to add. Do not build loss-framed streaks (no supporting evidence retrieved, and habit evidence favours tolerance of missed days), "items saved" or note-count progress metrics, or highlight and reread counters, all of which reward accumulation that the evidence rates low-yield. Do not have AI generate the whole plan or order, write summaries or notes, or answer before the learner attempts (Bastani et al. harm, [V]; REQ non-goals). Do not compete with free tutors on explanation. Do not sell the concept graph as a learning mechanism, since the shipped version is machine-derived and the retrieval-first evidence outranks it. Do not adopt CTDL, Open Badges, xAPI, LTI or ESCO for a single-learner local tool. Do not quote the FSRS benchmark numbers or the 2024 implementation-intention re-estimates until verified.

What to leave deferred, with the trigger for revisiting each. Modules and milestones, the `Outcome`, `OutcomeLink` and `Activity` tables, and a prerequisite DAG with a next-action query wait until usage shows learners are simulating modules with multiple goals or tags, and until there is evidence that learners can author prerequisites. AI curriculum drafting (the four-stage grounded pipeline) waits until the PRD's "Could" service-suggested reading is justified by usage data. If built, it should scope AI to critiquing the goal, generating retrieval prompts after an attempt, and verified-only resource suggestions, with the REQ tension resolved explicitly in the PRD and the volatile Crossref, OpenAlex and Open Library terms re-checked first. Suggested ordering on request waits until prior-knowledge capture exists, because of expertise reversal. Segment identification, multi-source corroboration, diagnosis and adaptation, themes, and the Open Badges, CASE and CTDL exports stay deferred. Multi-goal policy needs a decision: REQ discourages concurrent goals (FR-5) while the product permits unlimited goals with no status or archive, and the cheapest first step is an active, completed or archived goal state.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. The right comparison is not between Read to Learn and a competing curriculum builder, because none was verified. It is between the product's thin MVP, REQ's evidence-aligned long-term spec, and what the learning literature actually supports. REQ already encodes most of the supported mechanisms, while the PRD defers nearly all of them. The cheapest, best-evidenced additions are therefore to promote thin slices of REQ (goal scaffolding, a learner-defined completion criterion per question, one retrieval step, a pace-based time budget), not to add new curriculum machinery such as modules, prerequisite graphs or AI plans. That machinery has the least supporting evidence, the highest cost and the sharpest conflict with REQ's learner-authorship principle.

Two things should be resolved before any of this is built. The PRD must be brought back into line with the code, since it has stopped constraining scope, and the first usage signal (#21) must exist so that the product's own hypothesis can be tested. Without both, additions are guesses stacked on an unmeasured loop. The largest open uncertainty is demand: no evidence shows that time-poor adults will pay for a goal-scoped reading plan, and a competitor, or an adjacent product such as NotebookLM, may already cover more of it than this survey could establish.
