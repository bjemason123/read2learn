# Learning-science and instructional-design evidence for how an adult self-directed learner should define a personal curriculum

Evidence tags used on each finding (research date 2026-11-07):
- **[V]** = the core claim and numbers appeared in search-result text for the primary record (abstract-level) during this session.
- **[S]** = reported only via a secondary source (press, blog, citing paper); numbers need checking against the original before publication.
- **[B]** = background knowledge of the literature, NOT re-verified this session. The URL/DOI is given from memory and should be spot-checked.
- Strength labels: STRONG = meta-analysis or RCT; MODERATE = single controlled/field study or correlational at scale; WEAK = conceptual, opinion or practitioner lore.
- Research was limited (about 20 tool calls, mostly search snippets, few full-text fetches), so abstract-level numbers are the ceiling of verification.

## 1. Backward design, constructive alignment, revised Bloom: what do they imply for a learner defining their own curriculum, and is there evidence on learners writing their own objectives?

### Takeaway
The frameworks agree on the order: outcomes first, then evidence of achievement (assessment), then activities. They are design heuristics, not empirically validated interventions, so a tool should adopt them as light structure and not as proven efficacy. The most relevant causal-ish evidence on self-set goals is that goal specificity/level, goal setting and planning predict attainment in self-paced learning, and the evidence is mostly correlational.

### Cited Findings
- Wiggins & McTighe's Understanding by Design prescribes three backward stages: identify desired results, determine acceptable evidence, then plan learning experiences and instruction. WEAK as efficacy evidence (design framework, no RCT or meta-analysis located). [B] — Wiggins & McTighe (2005), *Understanding by Design*, 2nd ed., ASCD, https://www.ascd.org/books/understanding-by-design-expanded-2nd-edition
- Biggs' constructive alignment: intended learning outcomes are written as verbs of performance, and the teaching/learning activities and assessment tasks are aligned to those verbs. Evidence base is mainly case studies and institutional practice; WEAK to MODERATE. [B] — Biggs (1996), "Enhancing teaching through constructive alignment", *Higher Education* 32, 347-364, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00138871
- Revised Bloom's taxonomy recasts outcomes as a verb x knowledge-type grid (remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create; factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive). It is a classification aid; I did not locate strong empirical validation of a strict cumulative hierarchy. WEAK. [B] — Krathwohl (2002), "A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy: An Overview", *Theory Into Practice* 41(4), https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2
- In a field study of 4,831 learners in six MOOCs, goal setting and strategic planning were associated with attaining personal course goals, while help-seeking was associated with lower attainment. MODERATE (large n, correlational, self-reported SRL). [V] — Kizilcec, Pérez-Sanagustín & Maldonado (2017), *Computers & Education* 104, https://www.ing.uc.cl/?p=48917 (record listed in search results; the journal DOI is 10.1016/j.compedu.2016.10.001 [B])
- In a meta-analysis of self-regulated learning in work-related training and educational attainment (k = 430, N = 90,380), goal level, persistence, effort and self-efficacy had the strongest effects on learning, while planning, monitoring, help-seeking and emotion control showed no significant relationship with learning; the constructs explained about 17% of variance beyond ability and prior knowledge. STRONG (meta-analysis, but correlational primary studies, and "planning" was typically self-reported). [V] — Sitzmann & Ely (2011), *Psychological Bulletin* 137(3), 421-442, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/
- Goal-setting theory: specific, difficult goals produce higher performance than easy or "do your best" goals, moderated by commitment, feedback and task complexity. STRONG (large body of lab and field work), but it is mostly on performance tasks, not open-ended conceptual learning. [B] — Locke & Latham (2002), *American Psychologist* 57(9), 705-717, https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705
- Proximal (near-term, concrete) sub-goals raised self-efficacy, interest and skill relative to distal goals alone in children learning arithmetic. MODERATE (single experimental series, child sample). [B] — Bandura & Schunk (1981), *JPSP* 41(3), 586-598, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586

### Inferences
- The "goal + questions I want answered" structure in Read to Learn maps onto the outcomes stage of backward design. What it lacks is the second stage: a learner-defined evidence-of-achievement for each question (e.g. "I can explain X to a novice without notes", "I can answer these 5 self-written questions cold in 2 weeks"). Without it there is no way to know when a question is "answered".
- Sitzmann & Ely's null result for planning versus the positive result for goal level and Kizilcec's positive result for planning suggests that the value is in specific, challenging goals plus persistence and not in elaborate plan artefacts; a heavy planning UI should not be assumed to help.
- Revised Bloom's verbs are useful as a prompt vocabulary for writing observable outcomes ("explain", "compare", "apply", "critique") and as a check that a goal is not only "remember", but should be offered as optional language, not a gate.

### Gaps
- No experiment or meta-analysis found directly testing learners writing their own learning objectives against receiving objectives (for adult self-directed learners). Nearest evidence is the correlational MOOC goal-setting finding and the general goal-setting literature.
- No located empirical test of Understanding by Design as an intervention (effect sizes), or of strict Bloom hierarchy validity; claims here rest on conceptual sources.
- Latham & Locke findings on participative vs assigned goals were not retrieved this session.

## 2. Self-regulated learning (Zimmerman), self-directed learning readiness (Knowles, Grow), and the quality and effectiveness of learner-controlled planning and sequencing

### Takeaway
Self-regulated learning models (forethought, performance, reflection) are well established, but adults' unaided study decisions are often poor: they misjudge what they know and prefer low-yield strategies. A pooled analysis of learner control in educational technology found essentially zero average effect on achievement, so a tool should offer learner control paired with scaffolding (defaults, prompts, feedback), especially for low-prior-knowledge learners.

### Cited Findings
- Zimmerman's cyclical SRL model has three phases: forethought (task analysis, goal setting, planning, self-efficacy), performance (self-control, self-observation) and self-reflection (self-judgment, attributions, adaptive reactions). Conceptual model with extensive supporting research. [B] — Zimmerman (2002), "Becoming a Self-Regulated Learner: An Overview", *Theory Into Practice* 41(2), 64-70, https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2
- Updated meta-analysis of learner control within educational technology (18 peer-reviewed studies, 1996-2012): average effect close to zero (g = 0.05); the authors state there does not appear to be an advantage in giving the learner control over any particular instructional component; effects varied by age, setting and outcome type, with a larger effect in K-12 than college and a benefit for behavioural outcomes. STRONG-ish (meta-analysis, but only 18 studies and mostly computer-based instruction, not self-chosen curricula). [S] — Karich, Burns & Maki (2014), *Review of Educational Research* 84(3), https://www.edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05 ; ERIC record at https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/updated-meta-analysis-of-learner-control-within-educational-techn/fingerprints/
- A 2025 RCT preprint cites the learner-control literature as showing modest objective gains but higher engagement and satisfaction. [S] — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22526 (preprint)
- Learners often choose to restudy rather than self-test, and confidence driven by processing fluency can mismatch actual learning, producing overconfidence and sub-optimal allocation of study time. The cited sources are secondary summaries of Kornell & Bjork (2007) and Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013). MODERATE to STRONG (replicated lab and classroom work), but I did not read the originals. [S] — Kornell & Bjork (2007), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 14(2), 219-224, https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-012-0274-5.pdf (a related citing paper from the search results); Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013), *Annual Review of Psychology* 64, 417-444, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823 [B]
- In a field analysis of MOOC learners, help-seeking was associated with lower goal attainment, while goal setting and strategic planning were associated with higher attainment. MODERATE (correlational). [V] — Kizilcec et al. (2017), https://www.ing.uc.cl/?p=48917
- Grow's Staged Self-Directed Learning model proposes four learner stages (dependent, interested, involved, self-directed) and says teaching style should match the stage; a self-directed learner in a new subject may be "dependent" in that domain. WEAK to MODERATE (conceptual model, practitioner use). [B] — Grow (1991), "Teaching Learners to be Self-Directed", *Adult Education Quarterly* 41(3), 125-149, https://doi.org/10.1177/0001848191041003001
- Knowles' andragogy assumes adults are self-directing, experience-rich and goal/problem-oriented; critics (e.g. Brookfield, Candy) note SDL is a context-dependent skill rather than a trait and that readiness scales are weak predictors. Largely conceptual/WEAK as an efficacy claim. [B] — Knowles (1975), *Self-Directed Learning*, Association Press; Candy (1991), *Self-Direction for Lifelong Learning*, Jossey-Bass
- In a sample of students using a ChatGPT-style assistant while learning to code, students with weaker prior knowledge learned less, and self-perceived benefit exceeded actual benefit. PREPRINT, mixed observational and experimental. [S] — Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting (2024; v2 2025), arXiv:2409.09047, https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047

### Inferences
- Read to Learn's "manual ordering" gives pure learner control of sequencing. With a mean learner-control effect near zero and well-documented overconfidence, manual ordering alone is neither supported nor harmful per se; it is neutral on learning outcomes but likely positive on engagement/ownership. Scaffolds that have better support include self-test prompts and review scheduling (see section 5), prompts to state a prerequisite assumption, and a visible check on "what do I already know".
- Because Grow's and expertise-reversal logic both indicate that novices need more structure, the tool should adapt its scaffolding to a declared level of prior knowledge on the topic (e.g. "new to this" vs "experienced"), with less prompting for experienced learners (see section 4).
- "Help-seeking was negative" (Kizilcec) is probably a marker of struggling learners, not a causal effect, so do not read it as an argument against offering help.

### Gaps
- No direct study found on the quality of adults' self-generated reading sequences (versus expert-sequenced) in open-ended self-directed learning; nearly all learner-control evidence is in computer-based instruction courses.
- Tough's "adult learning projects" research (adults commonly run self-planned learning projects) and Knowles/Grow readiness-scale validity were not retrieved.
- Karich et al. details (sequencing as a separate moderator) were not available; the "no advantage on any component" quote is second-hand.

## 3. Goal-setting evidence: specific and proximal goals, mastery vs performance goals, implementation intentions, mental contrasting (WOOP), time-budgeting and the planning fallacy

### Takeaway
Specific, challenging, proximal goals and if-then plans have meaningful supporting evidence, but effect sizes shrink under bias correction (implementation intentions) and are small (about g = 0.3) for mental contrasting plus implementation intentions. People systematically underestimate time needed, so a tool should help the learner budget time using their own actual throughput data.

### Cited Findings
- Implementation intentions ("if situation X, then I will do Y"): 94 independent tests, d = 0.65 on goal attainment; most comparisons were against groups that had already formed a goal intention. STRONG, originally; subject to publication-bias concerns. [V] — Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69-119, https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes
- A later meta-analysis of 642 tests (Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, 2024) is reported to give much smaller bias-corrected estimates (d = 0.35 by trim-and-fill, d = 0.15 by robust Bayesian meta-analysis). The figures come from a secondary glossary page and conflict with the original d = 0.65; treat as unverified. [S] — https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions
- Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII, the basis of WOOP): 21 studies, 24 effects, N = 15,907, g = 0.336 (small to medium), with probable publication bias. STRONG-ish (meta-analysis) but modest and moderated by intervention style. [V] — Wang, Wang & Gai (2021), *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:565202, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full
- A health-focused meta-analysis reported g = 0.28 for the MCII combination. [S] — Cross & Sheffield (2019), cited in the same Wang et al. paper above.
- Planning prompts at the start of a HarvardX course raised completion by 29% and certificate payment by 40% (field experiment). MODERATE. [V] — Yeomans & Reich (2017), LAK17, https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137973
- A later multi-site scale-up of self-regulation interventions (including plan-making) raised engagement in the first weeks but not final completion; one earlier plan-making test showed a completion decline in a developed-world subgroup in one course (32% to 23%); a 2024 paper reports high variation in the effectiveness of planning tactics. MODERATE (large field trials). [V] — Kizilcec et al. (2020), *PNAS* 117(26), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459 (listed in results; summary taken from the search synthesis, check against the paper)
- Planning fallacy: students' predicted thesis completion time was substantially shorter than their actual time (my recollection: about 34 vs about 56 days; only around 30% finished by their predicted date). MODERATE (classic experimental/field). [B] — Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994), *JPSP* 67(3), 366-381, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366
- Habit formation: median time to approximate automaticity of about 66 days (range 18-254, with the long tail extrapolated); missing a single opportunity did not materially disrupt habit formation. MODERATE (n = 96, small, self-report). [V] — Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6), https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=8902 (secondary write-up); DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674 [B]
- Mastery (learning) vs performance goals: I did not retrieve a meta-analysis this session. Background: learning-goal orientation is generally associated with deeper strategy use and persistence, while performance-approach goals relate to grades; effects are modest and context dependent. WEAK (not retrieved). [B] — Hulleman, Schrager, Bodmann & Harackiewicz (2010), *Psychological Bulletin* 136(3), https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018947

### Inferences
- "Goal plus questions" is a good form for a specific learning-oriented (mastery) goal, but the tool should push toward proximal, time-bound sub-goals (e.g. "this week: answer question 2") because distal goals alone are weaker for sustaining progress.
- An optional "when/where will I read" if-then slot is well-supported and cheap, but expected effects are probably smaller than the 2006 figure; do not promise large gains.
- Because of the planning fallacy, the tool should compute estimated time-to-finish from the learner's observed past pace (reading items completed per week) rather than from a stated optimistic deadline, and show it as a range.
- A habit-formation framing implies no penalty for a missed day; long inconsistent gaps matter more than one lapse.

### Gaps
- No evidence found specifically on time-budgeting tools for reading lists; the planning-fallacy remedy ("outside view"/reference-class forecasting) was not searched.
- The 2024 bias-corrected implementation-intention estimates are unverified and the numbers look inconsistent between estimators; read the original before citing.
- No located evidence that WOOP/MCII helps in learning-specific (as opposed to health/behaviour) goals for adults.

## 4. Prerequisite structure and sequencing: knowledge space theory, prerequisite graphs, expertise reversal, scaffolding fading, and suggested orderings

### Takeaway
Prerequisite-aware sequencing is theoretically strong and is used in commercial adaptive systems, but I found no evidence that learners can reliably author an accurate prerequisite graph, and the expertise-reversal effect says the right amount of structure depends on prior knowledge. Suggested orderings should be offered as defaults the learner can override, with scaffolding that fades.

### Cited Findings
- Expertise reversal effect: instructional techniques that help inexperienced learners can lose effectiveness or even have negative consequences for more experienced learners. STRONG (many experiments; I did not retrieve a pooled effect size). [S] — Sweller, Ayres, Kalyuga & Chandler (2003) as described in search results; Kalyuga (2007), "Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction", *Educational Psychology Review* 19, 509-539, https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3
- Kalyuga, Rikers & Paas (2012) review expertise reversal effects across fields and their mechanisms; Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017) argue the effect is a special case of the element interactivity effect (a conceptual/empirical paper, not a meta-analysis). MODERATE. [S] — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3 (listed in results); Chen et al. (2017), *Educational Psychology Review* 29(2), 393-405
- Knowledge space theory models a domain as a set of items with a prerequisite (surface) relation, so that the learner's knowledge state is a feasible subset; it underlies the ALEKS adaptive system. Evidence is mainly psychometric and system-level; I did not retrieve efficacy RCTs. WEAK to MODERATE for this use. [B] — Doignon & Falmagne (1985), *International Journal of Man-Machine Studies* 23, 175-196; Falmagne, Albert, Doble, Eppstein & Hu (2013), *Knowledge Spaces: Applications in Education*, Springer, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-35329-1
- In the coding-course study, students with weaker prior knowledge learned less with unrestricted AI access and students with stronger prior knowledge benefited more, so prior knowledge moderates the benefit of open-ended help. PREPRINT. [S] — Lehmann et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047

### Inferences
- A suggested ordering is plausible (low risk) for novices but should be dropped or collapsed for experienced learners (expertise reversal). The tool should ask the learner about familiarity with the topic and tune default guidance accordingly.
- Letting learners draw prerequisite edges is not supported as a mechanism for correct sequencing (no evidence found that novices can do it accurately). The product's concept graph is better justified as a sense-making, review-prompt and gap-spotting aid than as a source of valid sequencing.
- Scaffolding should fade: more prompts early in a topic, fewer as the learner marks items as understood.

### Gaps
- No empirical evidence located on the accuracy of learner-authored prerequisite structures or on effects of "suggested reading order" tools.
- No pooled expertise-reversal effect size retrieved (the Chen et al. 2017 item is not a meta-analysis, contrary to my initial expectation).
- Scaffolding-fading evidence (e.g. faded worked examples) was not retrieved.

## 5. Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, and retrieval vs concept mapping vs note-taking vs learner-generated material: how strongly should a curriculum tool build review into the plan?

### Takeaway
Practice testing and spaced review are the best-supported techniques (rated high utility), and in head-to-head tests retrieval beat elaborative concept mapping. Learner-constructed concept maps do help relative to reading alone, but note-taking and highlighting are comparatively weak. Review scheduling and self-testing should be first-class in the plan, and the concept graph should not be sold as the main learning mechanism.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated ten techniques: practice testing and distributed practice "high" utility; summarization, rereading, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic and imagery for text "low"; the remaining three (my recollection: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice) "moderate". Concept mapping and note-taking were not among the ten rated. STRONG (comprehensive review, expert ratings, not a quantitative meta-analysis). [V for high/low sets; B for moderate set] — *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4-58, https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266
- Karpicke & Blunt (2011): in two experiments (200 students) studying science texts, retrieval practice produced about 50% better performance a week later than elaborative studying with concept mapping, including on inference questions and even when the final test required making concept maps; students predicted the opposite. STRONG (RCT-like lab experiments, undergraduates). [V] — *Science* 331(6018), 772-775, https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html ; DOI 10.1126/science.1199327 [B]; full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577217
- Testing effect meta-analysis (61 studies, 159 effect sizes): average g = 0.50 for testing vs restudy; larger with feedback (g = 0.73 vs 0.39 without) and with delayed final tests (g = 0.69 vs 0.41 same day), per secondary summaries. STRONG. [S] — Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6), 1432-1463, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559 (PDF located at the EPFL courseware link in results)
- Distributed practice: quantitative synthesis of 839 assessments in 317 experiments (verbal recall tasks); spacing benefit and optimal lag depends on retention interval. STRONG for verbal material; I did not retrieve a pooled effect size. [V] — Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354-380, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/
- Interleaving meta-analysis (59 studies, 238 effects): overall g = 0.42; strongest for visual category learning (painting styles, g = 0.67) and reversed for word-based materials (g = -0.39), so benefit depends on similarity of the categories. STRONG, but domain-specific; the relevance for reading-list ordering is uncertain. [V] — Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11), 1029-1052, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/
- Concept-map meta-analysis (2017): 142 effect sizes, overall g = 0.58; creating concept maps g = 0.72 vs studying given maps g = 0.43. STRONG for map-vs-control comparisons, but most controls were reading/lecture or other lower-activity conditions, not retrieval practice. [S] — Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30, 431-455, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9 (figures reported in the search synthesis)
- Earlier concept/knowledge-map meta-analysis: 55 studies, 67 effect sizes, N = 5,818; secondary reports of g about 0.82 for map construction and about 0.37-0.4 for studying maps (not verified against the paper). STRONG, numbers unverified. [S] — Nesbit & Adesope (2006), *Review of Educational Research* 76(3), 413-448, https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm
- Learner-generated vs provided material: generating one's own responses improves memory relative to reading (generation effect), and creating maps beat studying given maps in Schroeder et al.; the generation effect literature is robust but largely word-level. STRONG at the generation-effect level. [B] — Slamecka & Graf (1978), *J. Exp. Psychol: Human Learning & Memory* 4(6), 592-604, https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592; Bertsch et al. (2007) meta-analysis, *Memory & Cognition* 35, 201-210
- Karpicke has noted concept mapping can be reframed as a retrieval-practice activity (drawing the map from memory) as an area being explored, not a settled result. [S] — https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html

### Inferences
- Read to Learn's notes with tags and the concept graph are generative, which is positive versus passive highlighting, but should not be assumed to produce retention comparable to retrieval. The strongest add is an in-product "recall prompt": after finishing an item, ask the learner to write the answer to one of their stated questions without looking, then show their notes for feedback. Feedback roughly doubles the testing effect (g 0.73 vs 0.39, secondary).
- Review should be scheduled into the plan (spacing at increasing intervals) and attached to the learner's own questions, not just to flashcard-style facts. Interleaving gains are material-dependent (positive for category discrimination, negative for word lists), so do not make mixing topics a default for reading-list ordering.
- If the concept graph is retained, a "redraw from memory" mode (retrieval-based concept mapping) would align it with the best evidence; I found no direct trial of that for adult readers.
- Collapse of ratings: summarization, rereading and highlighting are rated low utility, so features that encourage them (e.g. "read again" counters or highlight-count progress) would be unsupported.

### Gaps
- No pooled effect for retrieval-based concept mapping vs retrieval practice (head-to-head replication beyond Karpicke & Blunt not retrieved).
- Nesbit & Adesope (2006) effect sizes and Rowland moderator numbers were taken from secondary sources.
- Specific meta-analytic evidence for note-taking (encoding vs external storage) and for tagging/linking notes was not retrieved.

## 6. The "collector's fallacy", reading-list accumulation, and completion/abandonment of self-paced learning (MOOC completion, habits, streaks)

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed study of the "collector's fallacy" (it is practitioner lore). Indirect evidence supports the concern: self-paced online learning has very low completion, learners misjudge their own mastery, and planning prompts help engagement but scale-up completion effects are inconsistent. Streak mechanics have no direct evidence located, and habit research suggests flexibility rather than strict streaks.

### Cited Findings
- "Collector's fallacy" (saving or tagging material feels like learning but is not): practitioner concept, not a research construct; no empirical test found. WEAK. [B] — Tietze (2013), https://zettelkasten.de/posts/collectors-fallacy/ (URL from memory, unverified)
- MOOC completion: a six-year data set (2012-2018, 12.6 million enrolments) shows course completion rates generally below 10% with no clear improvement, according to a secondary summary of the paper. MODERATE (large data, but MOOC audiences mostly do not intend to complete). [S] — Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente (2019), "The MOOC pivot", *Science* 363(6423), 130-131, https://sandwalk.blogspot.com/2020/01/remember-moocs.html?m=1 (blog summary; original not retrieved)
- Learners' stated intentions matter: in MOOCs, goal setting and strategic planning predict personal-goal attainment, which suggests that raw completion understates success for those who never meant to finish. MODERATE. [V] — Kizilcec et al. (2017), https://www.ing.uc.cl/?p=48917
- Planning prompts: +29% completion and +40% certificate payment in one field experiment, but multi-site scale-up improved early engagement, not final completion, with possible harm in a developed-world subgroup in one course. MODERATE. [V] — https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137973 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459
- Habit formation: median about 66 days, wide range, a single missed day did not significantly matter, but highly inconsistent performers did not form habits. MODERATE. [V] — Lally et al. (2010), https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=8902
- Learners overrate their own understanding when material feels fluent and often choose restudy over self-testing, which is the mechanism by which accumulating and re-reading material can feel productive. MODERATE to STRONG. [S] — Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013) and Kornell & Bjork (2007) via secondary summaries cited in section 2.

### Inferences
- The product's "deferral/backlog" is defensible as a way of not letting the active plan grow without bound, but the evidence supports limiting active work in progress and tying each item to one of the learner's questions rather than allowing unlimited unattached items.
- A backlog count or "items saved" metric is not evidence of learning; completion should be defined via recall or answered questions (section 1 evidence-of-achievement).
- Streaks are not supported by anything I retrieved; the habit evidence favours tolerance of missed days and cue-based if-then reminders. Streak-loss messaging risks extrinsic pressure (no evidence located either way).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed research found on reading-list accumulation, "read later" abandonment or the collector's fallacy.
- Jordan (2015) MOOC completion figures and the Science paper itself were not retrieved; numbers are second-hand.
- No evidence located on streak mechanics for learning (e.g. Duolingo-type studies) or on whether backlog size predicts abandonment.

## 7. AI-assisted learning and curriculum generation (2023-2026): what an AI should and should not do in a curriculum tool

### Takeaway
Unrestricted LLM access boosts immediate task performance but harms later unaided performance, while guardrailed tutors that withhold full answers largely remove the harm and, in one RCT, outperformed in-class active learning. The evidence therefore supports an AI that scaffolds (hints, questions, retrieval prompts) and does not do the learner's thinking, and I found no evidence on AI-generated curricula as such.

### Cited Findings
- Field experiment, nearly 1,000 high-school maths students: unrestricted GPT-4 access ("GPT Base") improved practice performance by 48%; a guardrailed tutor ("GPT Tutor") by 127%; when access was removed, the GPT Base group scored 17% worse on the exam than students who never had access, while the negative effect was largely mitigated for GPT Tutor. MODERATE-STRONG (randomized field experiment; one school in Turkey, one subject, four sessions). Peer reviewed. [V] — Bastani et al. (2025), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics", *PNAS* 122(26), e2422633122, https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html ; DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422633122 [B]; preprint https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486
- Randomized crossover trial, 194 Harvard physics students: an AI tutor instructed to give one step at a time rather than the full solution produced more than double the median learning gains of in-class active learning, in less time (reported median about 49 vs 75 minutes), with higher self-reported engagement and motivation. MODERATE (peer reviewed; elite sample; one topic pair; authors caution against generalization). [S] — Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti (2025), *Scientific Reports* 15, 17458, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/
- LLM use increased learning outcomes when students asked for explanations, and hampered learning when they asked for solutions to practice exercises; weaker-prior-knowledge students learned less; self-perceived benefit exceeded actual benefit. PREPRINT (arXiv, observational plus experimental). [S] — Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting (2024; v2 2025), https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047
- "Metacognitive laziness": ChatGPT use improved immediate task performance while promoting dependence on the tool and possibly undermining long-term learning without scaffolding; the work suggests tasks that require active engagement with AI feedback. Peer reviewed (BJET) but I only saw secondary descriptions; sample and effect sizes unverified. [S] — Fan et al. (2024/2025), *British Journal of Educational Technology* 56(2), 489-530, DOI 10.1111/bjet.13544, preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09315
- Essay-writing study (54 participants in sessions 1-3, 18 in session 4): LLM users showed the weakest EEG connectivity, lowest ownership of their essays, and difficulty quoting their own work; authors say findings are context-dependent. PREPRINT, small, not peer reviewed at the time of retrieval; weak evidence about learning per se. [S] — Kosmyna et al. (2025), arXiv:2506.08872, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872
- A 2025 RCT preprint on an AI instructional agent examines perceived learner control and outcomes (details not retrieved). PREPRINT. [S] — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22526

### Inferences
- Supported AI roles for a curriculum tool: (a) ask the learner to articulate goals, questions and evidence-of-achievement and critique them (vague, too broad, mismatched to the verb level); (b) generate retrieval prompts and quiz the learner on their own questions, giving feedback after the learner attempts; (c) give hints and explanations on request after an attempt; (d) flag time estimates against the learner's real pace.
- Unsupported or risky AI roles: generating the whole curriculum and reading order on the learner's behalf (it removes the forethought phase that SRL treats as the learner's skill, and no evidence shows AI-generated sequences improve outcomes); summarizing readings in place of reading them; writing the learner's notes or answers (the "crutch" and "metacognitive laziness" pattern); giving answers before the learner attempts retrieval.
- Because learners over-estimate the benefit of AI help (Lehmann et al.) and low-prior-knowledge learners are the most at risk, guardrails should default on, and any "just give me the answer" path should be explicit and logged as such.
- If the AI suggests items or orderings, they should be labelled as suggestions with reasons, and the learner should decide (consistent with learner-control scaffolding in section 2).

### Gaps
- No evidence located on LLM-generated curricula or reading lists versus self- or expert-designed ones; claims here are inferences.
- No AI-and-learning meta-analysis was retrieved in this session (published 2025 meta-analyses of ChatGPT effects exist but were not verified, and are likely dominated by short, low-quality studies); check before citing any pooled figure.
- Several 2024-2026 items are preprints (flagged) and the Kestin and Fan details come from secondary summaries.

## 8. Synthesis: what a curriculum-definition tool must include to help an adult self-directed learner, and which common features are unsupported or harmful

### Takeaway
Best-supported must-haves are specific learning goals with learner-defined evidence of achievement, proximal sub-goals with realistic time estimates, built-in retrieval practice with spacing and feedback, and prior-knowledge-sensitive scaffolding with learner control retained. Weakly supported or risky features are strict streaks, unlimited saving of items, autonomous AI curriculum generation, highlight/re-read mechanics, and treating a concept graph or note volume as proof of learning.

### Cited Findings
- Must-have 1, outcomes plus evidence: goal level and persistence had the strongest self-regulation effects on learning in a 430-study meta-analysis; goal setting predicted goal attainment in 4,831 MOOC learners. — [Sitzmann & Ely 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401218/); [Kizilcec et al. 2017](https://www.ing.uc.cl/?p=48917)
- Must-have 2, retrieval and spaced review: practice testing and distributed practice were the only two techniques rated high utility; testing effect average g = 0.50 (secondary); retrieval beat concept mapping by about 50% at one week. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266); [Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559); [Karpicke & Blunt 2011](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html)
- Must-have 3, if-then plans (optional): d = 0.65 originally, likely lower after bias correction (unverified). — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)
- Must-have 4, scaffolded learner control: learner control alone has about zero average effect (g = 0.05); prior knowledge moderates benefit of open-ended help and structure. — [Karich et al. 2014 via EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05); [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- Must-have 5, guardrailed AI: unguarded GPT-4 led to a 17% lower exam score than no access; guardrailed tutor largely mitigated the harm. — [Bastani et al. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html)
- Unsupported or low-yield: rereading, highlighting and summarization rated low utility. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)
- Weak or unverified: streaks (no evidence retrieved), collector's fallacy (practitioner concept only), prerequisite graphs authored by novices (no evidence retrieved). — see sections 4 and 6.
- Concept mapping is effective relative to lower-activity controls (g = 0.58 overall, creating g = 0.72), so the concept graph is supported as a generative activity but not as a substitute for retrieval. — [Schroeder et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9)
- Comparison point for the Read to Learn feature set: goal plus questions (supported, if proximal and with evidence-of-achievement added); reading items and progress states (supported only as bookkeeping; completion of reading is not evidence of learning); notes with tags (generative, moderate); deferral/backlog (reasonable if capped); manual ordering (neutral on outcomes, likely positive on engagement); concept graph (generative, plausible but not the best-evidenced mechanism).

### Inferences
- Gap analysis against the product (inferred, not evidence from a single source): missing an evidence-of-achievement field per question; missing retrieval prompts and spaced review linked to the learner's questions; missing realistic time-estimate/pace feedback; missing a prior-knowledge flag to modulate scaffolding; missing guardrailed AI roles.
- Features that could be harmful if added: gamified streaks with loss framing, "items saved" or "notes count" progress metrics that reward accumulation, AI that auto-generates the whole plan or writes summaries/answers, and unrestricted answer-giving chat.
- Where evidence is weakest and product decisions are therefore judgement calls: the value of the concept graph, the value of manual ordering versus suggested ordering, and whether an AI-suggested reading list helps adult learners.

### Gaps
- Almost no evidence is specific to adult self-directed learners reading their own curated material; most evidence comes from students in courses, MOOCs or lab studies, so transfer is an assumption.
- This research relied on abstract-level and secondary sources for many numbers; tags above show which. Items marked [B] should be verified before publication.
